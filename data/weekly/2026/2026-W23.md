## The week in 90 seconds

**The week the frontier-lab race became a public-market test — and Microsoft cut the cord**

This was the week the AI boom's central question stopped being "how good is the model?" and started being "who can afford it, who governs it, and what is it worth?" Anthropic started the first frontier-lab IPO clock, Microsoft turned from OpenAI's biggest customer into its rival, open weights reached the coding frontier, and a UK regulator handed down the first binding order on how generative search may consume the open web.

The defining move came Monday. Anthropic confidentially filed a draft S-1 with the SEC — the first pure-play frontier lab to formally begin going public — days after closing a $65B Series H at a roughly $965B valuation on a reported ~$47B revenue run-rate, nearly tripling February's $380B mark and overtaking OpenAI. A confidential filing stays private for 60-to-90 days, so the public prospectus surfaces in late summer, with OpenAI reported just behind on a September target above $1 trillion. For the first time the frontier order faces a real public-market verdict on whether revenue and margins justify trillion-dollar-adjacent prices — and Hacker News treated it as exactly that, splitting valuation skepticism from capability respect, with the Economist's "can the stockmarket swallow them?" and an "S&P 500 rejects SpaceX, OpenAI and Anthropic" thread (1,434 points) anchoring a bubble debate that by Sunday had curdled into open polarization rather than uniform doubt.

The structural shift underneath was Microsoft's. At Build 2026, Satya Nadella unveiled Project Polaris — Microsoft's own coding model running on its Maia 200 silicon, set to replace GPT-4 Turbo as the default GitHub Copilot engine for every subscriber by August. Around it came seven first-party MAI models (MAI-Thinking-1 claims roughly 10x output-tokens per dollar versus GPT-5.5), the MIT-licensed Windows Agent Framework 1.0, and a preview of Azure Agent Mesh. Microsoft now owns the model, the inference silicon, and the developer surface end-to-end — a public renegotiation of tech's most important partnership, framed explicitly around cutting developer cost. That cost theme ran all week: GitHub Copilot moved every plan to metered "AI Credits" on June 1, and the loudest dev signal wasn't a release but the bill — Uber's $1,500-per-engineer monthly cap, "$1,500 to hack an app," and Sam Altman conceding token costs are "an issue."

The third thread was about who controls the enterprise-agent runtime. The week opened as a tollbooth fight — SAP blocking noncompliant external agents starting June 9, Salesforce's headless MCP-first "Headless 360," ServiceNow's GA "Action Fabric" — then inverted when NVIDIA's enterprise Agent Toolkit knit 17 rival platforms (Salesforce, SAP, ServiceNow, Red Hat, Palantir, Adobe, Siemens, CrowdStrike, Atlassian) onto one governed runtime. The lesson: control is migrating from the model to the runtime and data plane, and NVIDIA just tightened its grip there. Meanwhile a wall of model releases — Claude Opus 4.8 (now default, with dynamic workflows orchestrating fleets of background agents), Gemini 3.5 Flash GA out-coding last quarter's Pro at $1.50/$9, GPT-5.3-Codex, Apache-licensed Mistral Small 4 — was capped by MiniMax M3, the first open-weight model to fuse frontier coding (59% SWE-Bench Pro), a 1M-token window and native multimodality. Open weights now squeeze closed labs from below just as Gemini Flash squeezes price from the side.

On governance, commentary became enforcement. The UK CMA issued a world-first binding order forcing Google to let publishers opt out of AI search for both grounding and training with no down-ranking penalty — a template other regulators will copy — while the EU's Digital Omnibus deal slipped high-risk AI Act obligations to December 2027 even as Article 50 transparency rules stay live for August 2, 2026, and Connecticut signed a second companion-AI law.

**Read this first:** [Open and closed models are on different exponentials](https://www.interconnects.ai/p/open-and-closed-models-are-on-different) — Nathan Lambert's piece, the week's single most-cited source, is the clearest frame for why this week's open-vs-closed, IPO, and cost stories are all the same story.

## Top papers
- **Capability Advertisement as a Market for Lemons: A Trust Layer for Heterogeneous Agent Networks** (arXiv:2606.03034) — Gaurav Naresh Mittal. _why notable:_ argues MCP/A2A registries naively treat advertised agent capabilities as static and truthful, creating adverse selection; a direct theoretical counter to "just open the MCP server" enthusiasm. [arxiv.org/abs/2606.03034](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.03034)
- **Proof-Carrying Agent Actions (PCAA)** (arXiv:2606.04104) — _why notable:_ model-agnostic runtime governance that attaches verifiable proofs to agent actions so policies are enforced across mixed stacks regardless of model — a concrete agent-governance primitive. [arxiv.org/abs/2606.04104](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.04104)
- **The Evaluation Blind Spot: A Stereological Theory of Benchmark Coverage for LLMs** (arXiv:2606.05169) — Jason Z Wang. _why notable:_ LLM leaderboards have low effective dimensionality (2.9–4.8); 92% of random visible/held-out splits flip the top-1 ranking — a structural blind spot two orders of magnitude larger than the runner-up gap. [arxiv.org/abs/2606.05169](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.05169)
- **Cascading Hallucination in Agentic RAG: The CHARM Framework** (arXiv:2606.04435) — Saroj Mishra et al. _why notable:_ characterizes how a single retrieval hallucination propagates and amplifies across multi-step agentic RAG, with a detection-plus-mitigation framework to break the cascade. [arxiv.org/abs/2606.04435](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.04435)
- **Memory is Reconstructed, Not Retrieved: Graph Memory for LLM Agents** (arXiv:2606.06036) — Ji, Li, Hooi et al. _why notable:_ argues agent memory should be reconstructed from a graph rather than retrieved verbatim, improving long-horizon recall — the anchor of a strong week for agent-memory work. [arxiv.org/abs/2606.06036](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.06036)
- **Toward Pre-Deployment Assurance for Enterprise AI Agents** (arXiv:2606.04037) — Thanh Luong Tuan, Abhijit Sanyal. _why notable:_ ontology-grounded verification with an Agent Operational Envelope and machine-verifiable Trust Certificate; ontology-driven scenario generation beats persona baselines on regulatory coverage (48.3% vs 33.1%) across four regulated industries. [arxiv.org/abs/2606.04037](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.04037)
- **What Benchmarks Don't Measure: Evaluating Abstention Competence in Autonomous Agents** (arXiv:2606.02965) — Ojewale, Venkatasubramanian. _why notable:_ names "compliance bias" — RLHF-trained agents structurally tend to proceed even without sufficient inputs/authorization; argues evals must measure whether an agent *should* have acted. [arxiv.org/abs/2606.02965](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.02965)
- **When Helping Hurts: Multi-Agent Debate for Data Cleaning** (arXiv:2606.02866) — Chirag Parmar et al. _why notable:_ across 4 model families and 6,000+ conditions, debate's sign reverses — degrading generation (−1.6 to −15.5pp) via "critique-induced confusion" while improving error detection — a useful corrective to multi-agent-debate hype. [arxiv.org/abs/2606.02866](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.02866)
- **Attested Tool-Server Admission: A Security Extension to MCP** (arXiv:2605.24248) — _why notable:_ attestation-based admission control so MCP hosts verify tool servers before granting access — directly relevant to MCP supply-chain risk as the spec consolidates. [arxiv.org/abs/2605.24248](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.24248)
- **Agent Skills for LLMs: Architecture, Acquisition, Security, and the Path Forward** (arXiv:2602.12430) — _why notable:_ synthesizes the emerging "agent skills" stack (SKILL specs, autonomous discovery, skill-level security) into a research agenda — directly mirrors the skills monoculture dominating GitHub trending all week. [arxiv.org/abs/2602.12430](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.12430)
