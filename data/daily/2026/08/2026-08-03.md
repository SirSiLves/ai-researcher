## The day in 90 seconds

**Brussels opens AI Act enforcement on cyberattacks while Washington finishes its framework and won't publish it**

The first real enforcement conversations under the AI Act are about cyber incidents connected to frontier models, not the deepfake labels most compliance teams spent the year preparing for. The same day, the White House confirmed it met its 1 August deadline for a frontier-evaluation framework and declined to say what is in it, who has seen it, or when labs start using it. Two governance regimes, one week, moving in opposite directions on transparency.

Day two of Article 50 applicability made the shape of European enforcement clearer, and it is not what the compliance playbooks anticipated. The Commission has opened discussions with OpenAI and Anthropic about cyberattacks connected to their models, following Anthropic's decision to grant ENISA access to Mythos. Lawyers reading GPAI liability now put procedural conduct inside the fineable surface: refusing an information request, giving misleading answers, or blocking a model evaluation is actionable on its own, and providers outside the EU must designate a local representative. The Commission's own explainer sets the ceilings at €15M or 3% of turnover for companies against €750k for EU institutions, and a public complaint tool plus a confidential whistleblower channel are already live. Washington produced the mirror image: voluntary cyber tests measuring the hacking capability of the most advanced US models, run out of the Office of the National Cyber Director, contents undisclosed.

Security was the densest thread on the board, and it moved on both sides at once. Google disclosed that Gemini-driven agents found, triaged and patched 1,072 Chrome security bugs in 60 days — fixes per milestone climbed from roughly 50 through M145 to 350 in M148, and the agents surfaced a decade-old flaw human reviewers had repeatedly missed. Chrome is now weighing a twice-weekly patch cycle because shipping fixes, not finding bugs, has become the constraint. Jack Clark's Import AI describes the other direction: a research worm that compromises machines and then runs an open-weight model on the GPUs it steals, with no reliance on vendor APIs that could be monitored or revoked, at roughly 37% end-to-end success. Interpol put an aggregate number on it — AI involved in 55% of reported cybercrime across 36 African countries, with losses rising from $192M to $484M. Capital agrees on where the problem sits: Zenity raised $125M, Horizon3 took $250M at a $2B valuation, and Visa paid $2.4B for BioCatch, all in one day.

Underneath both stories is a measurement problem that got worse today. A validity audit of agent-safety benchmarks found that a trivially "always positive" policy scores F1 0.690 on R-Judge, beating five of 21 models that genuinely discriminate, and that three widely cited benchmarks rank the same 18 models differently — with capability correlating negatively with safety scores. Separately, ten institutions including the UK AI Security Institute handed a frontier model the central research question from two unpublished NeurIPS submissions and asked the real authors to grade the output: Reject and Strong Reject, with near-identical criticisms. Excellent engineers, poor researchers is the crispest statement of that asymmetry yet, and it arrives in the same week both Brussels and Washington propose to govern these systems primarily through evaluation. What to watch tomorrow: frontier labs are at the White House on Tuesday to review the framework nobody outside the room has read.

## Research highlights

- **[Safety, or Just Capability? A Validity Audit of Agent-Safety Benchmarks](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.28685)** — Wang, Han, Shang et al. Runs R-Judge, InjecAgent, AgentHarm and AgentDojo under their official scorers on up to 22 models and finds the metrics don't hold: a trivially "always positive" policy scores F1 0.690 on R-Judge, the three broad benchmarks rank the same 18 models differently, and capability predicts task success (ρ=+0.60) while correlating *negatively* with safety scores. arXiv 2607.28685.
- **[Tool Specifications Matter: Uncovering and Mitigating Safety Risks in AI Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.29254)** — Pan, Yang, Yuan et al. Pins agent safety degradation on the schema-formatted tool spec itself — white-box analysis shows JSON schemas weaken the model's internal refusal signal. SafeKeep decouples the paths (judge on flattened text specs, execute on the original schema), lifting harmful-request refusal from 23.8% to 70.6% across four LLMs. The interop finding of the day.
- **[Memory Provenance Laundering in LLM Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.29167)** — Xu, Xiao, Shao et al. LLM-based memory consolidation rewrites an untrusted external observation as apparent user history, preserving the action trigger while erasing the low-trust source. Laundered memories reach a 1.000 attack success rate; a provenance-preserving middleware matching action risk to memory authority blocks every evaluated unauthorized high-risk action.
- **[Beyond Component Testing: Validating Agentic AI Systems](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.29405)** — Mirto, D'Agati, Tricomi et al. Synthesises 257 papers into a five-dimension taxonomy (behavioral, safety, temporal, regulatory, multi-agent). Verdict: behavioral evaluation is comparatively mature; temporal validity, runtime evidence maintenance, regulatory legibility and open-ended multi-agent assurance are not. The gap map is the useful part.
- **[Benchmarks Are Not Validation: A System-Level View of Financial LLM Applications](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.28840)** — Payzun, Demirtaş, Scala et al. Written from inside financial institutions actually validating GenAI systems: benchmark scores are not production readiness for stacks combining retrieval, proprietary data, tool use, orchestration, monitoring and human escalation. Lists the controls LLM-as-judge needs — multiple judges, rubrics, agreement, auditability.
- **[GoldenRetriever: Non-Interactive Homomorphic Encrypted Retrieval for Privacy-Preserving RAG](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.29019)** — Gao, Quan, Piersall et al. Drops encrypted top-k ranking for threshold selection, cutting retrieval cost from quadratic to linear in corpus size. CKKS-based, fully non-interactive, leaks neither the query, the intermediate similarity scores, nor which documents were selected.
- **[Reproducing LightMem: Naive RAG Is Just as Good for Memory Management](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.29104)** — Zhou, Wang, Koopman et al. Swapping only the retriever over a fixed memory store moves answer accuracy 58.1% → 75.5%. Retriever choice dominates the memory architecture; constructed memories don't consistently beat raw-turn retrieval.
- **[AISPA: User-Centric System Prompt Auditing](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.28617)** — Lin, Zhu, Yang et al. Audits 3,249 instructions from the system prompts of 88 commercial AI products along eight user-facing dimensions, labelling each protective or problematic. A transparency artifact for a layer deployed everywhere and disclosed to neither users nor regulators.

Also worth a look: **[Hypothetical Prompt Embeddings](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.29402)** moves HyDE-style generation from query time to index time (question-question matching, zero added query latency), **[QASP](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.29606)** predicts the full per-query recall curve in one upfront regression rather than iterating during search, and **[Mixture-of-Translators](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.28979)** learns to map one model's KV cache into another's — cross-model cache reuse as an interoperability primitive.

## Best blog reads

- **[Import AI 467: Self-sustaining AI viruses; pacing AI progress; confusion about AI and creativity](https://jack-clark.net/2026/08/03/import-ai-467-self-sustaining-ai-viruses-pacing-ai-progress-confusion-about-ai-and-creativity/)** — Jack Clark. Three findings that should be read together. (1) A worm from Toronto/Vector/Cambridge/ServiceNow compromises machines then uses the stolen GPUs to run an open-weight LLM for its own reasoning — "no reliance on vendor APIs that could be monitored or revoked" — via a scoped reasoning graph that limits context growth. ~80% vulnerability detection → ~53% exploitation → 88% self-replication, so ~37% end-to-end. Resilience comes from swarming, not reliability. (2) The shadow evaluation: ten institutions including the UK AI Security Institute handed Claude Opus 4.8 the central research question from two unpublished NeurIPS 2026 submissions; the real authors graded the output Reject and Strong Reject, with identical criticisms. (3) Dwarkesh Patel's argument that compute gets *more* expensive — a true human-level SWE on an H100-equivalent implies that H100 should rent for over $250k/year, ~15x today's spot.
- **[Devtools must be open source](https://blog.exe.dev/devtools-must-be-open-source)** — David Crawshaw. Turns the tired freedom argument into something operational. The freedom to modify your tools was theoretical because learning a codebase cost more than the change was worth; agents collapse that on two fronts, and the second is the one nobody noticed — an agent can *maintain the fork*, rebasing your local changes on upstream nightly. His conclusion: "the source code is the extension system." Plugin APIs existed to amortize the cost of change across many users; if that cost is near zero for one user, the extension point is probably the wrong shape.
- **[If AI Outputs Aren't Speech, Who Has to Prove They're Human?](https://www.lawfaremedia.org/article/if-ai-outputs-aren-t-speech--who-has-to-prove-they-re-human)** — Becca Branum, Lawfare. Grants the doctrinal question — a growing body of scholarship argues LLM output isn't First Amendment speech — and asks the administrative one: if human attribution is the line, someone has to sort human from machine text at scale, and no tool can. Classifiers answer the wrong question; provenance shows a tool touched a file, not that a person adopted the words. The cost lands on users and readers, as reduced anonymity and identity checks used as a proxy for authorship.
- **[The Economics of Chinese LLM: Pricing, Unit Economics, and the Competitive Endgame](https://chierhu.medium.com/the-economics-of-chinese-llm-pricing-unit-economics-and-the-competitive-endgame-b04fc5aab128)** — Chier Hu. The binding constraint on Chinese cost floors is now HBM supply, not logic fabrication — and the order-of-magnitude price claim holds cleanly at the token level, not the task level (reasoning models emit far more output tokens; Anthropic's tokenizer emits ~30% more for the same text). The line to remember: "Chinese inference is not structurally unprofitable — it is deliberately under-monetized."
- **[The AI Agent Reported 14 Green Checks. Acceptance Still Failed.](https://dmytronasyrov.medium.com/the-ai-agent-reported-14-green-checks-acceptance-still-failed-8dd5320d4da7)** — Dmytro Nasyrov. Same model family, same six utilities, two information conditions: the draft-first session reported 14/14 smoke checks passed and then scored 7/13 against an independent frozen suite it had never seen. "Those counts had different denominators and different finish lines." Honest about the wider literature cutting both ways.
- **[Introducing our Artifacts Hub and Adoption Dashboard](https://www.interconnects.ai/p/introducing-our-artifacts-hub-and)** — Nathan Lambert. 792 models over two years with distance-from-frontier, HF vs OpenRouter adoption, size-normalized download scores, plus a daily-updating adoption dashboard by geography and organization. The measurement layer the open-vs-closed policy fight has been arguing without — published the same day a [Medium piece argued adoption dashboards systematically lie](https://medium.com/@vikram.vishal/your-ai-adoption-dashboard-is-lying-to-you-5c108bd62ea9).
