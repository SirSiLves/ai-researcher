## The day in 90 seconds

**OpenAI cut Luna 80 percent, and a controlled study handed retrieval back to BM25**

OpenAI dropped list prices on two of its three GPT-5.6 tiers and now claims year-ago frontier-class performance at roughly six cents on the dollar per task. The same morning, the most carefully controlled retrieval comparison yet published found that a keyword algorithm from the 1990s sits on the cost-accuracy frontier at every corpus size tested. The two stories arrive at one conclusion: the expensive parts of an AI stack are increasingly not the parts doing the work.

The cut is 80 percent on Luna and 20 percent on Terra, with Sol holding its price but gaining a Fast mode — up to 2.5× standard speed at twice the cost, no change in intelligence — in place of Priority Processing. The same rates govern how usage draws down Codex and ChatGPT Work subscriptions, so this is a bill change for existing customers rather than a new SKU. OpenAI's own benchmark claim is the aggressive one: on Agents' Last Exam, Luna outperforms Fable 5 at an estimated cost per task nearly 99 percent lower. Hacker News read it as a break in the plateau narrative rather than a discount — "I genuinely thought we were in a stage where we were plateauing," ran the top comment, while another credited the competitive set, Kimi K3 and GLM 5.2, by name. The sharpest dissent was more useful than the enthusiasm: cheap tiers buy nothing if you cannot separate trivial work from hard work, and routing is unsolved. The market seems to agree. OmniRoute, a model-routing layer, took its third consecutive four-digit star day and is growing about 30 percent week over week.

The retrieval paper is the quieter story and the more expensive one to ignore. Across 28 strictly nested corpus tiers, from 1,000 to 512,000 documents, with questions, reader and judge held constant, BM25 defines the low-cost Pareto edge at every tier and leads on accuracy from mid-scale upward — with no LLM construction step at all. The File-System Agent spends 39 times more query tokens and finishes roughly 20 points behind at full scale, but swap its retrieval substrate for BM25 and it scores 69.4 against 36.9, which locates the failure in the plumbing rather than in agency. Graph RAG burns up to 24.6 generated tokens per indexed corpus token and still stalls inside the first two percent of the corpus. The practitioner writing landed the same day on the same side: an MCP extension called Token Saver claims 90 to 99 percent savings on document-heavy calls by serving retrieved spans instead of whole files, LlamaIndex shipped the first parsing benchmark for the OCR layer all of this sits on, and one engineer published six months of running RAG on Postgres and pgvector after a five-figure Pinecone invoice.

The counterweight is that the security bill is moving the other way. Okta agreed to buy Permiso for about $200 million, days after Cyera paid $1 billion for Oasis Security — two nine-figure bets in one week on the premise that verifying an identity at login is worthless against an agent that authenticates legitimately and then behaves unexpectedly for four days. The day's disclosure gives that premise a face: the first prompt injection engineered to copy itself into the document Copilot for Word produces, reported to Microsoft 144 days ago with no mitigation for the class, and 287 comments of resigned recognition on Hacker News. OpenAI's own answer, GPT-Red, is honest about the economics — the single largest documented LLM safety training run, an attacker model trained against a population of defenders, now finding more successful attacks than human red-teamers, and the model it hardened is the one whose price fell today. Injection robustness has become something you buy with compute rather than patch, which is why AI security is the fastest-moving topic on the radar at plus 38.8 percent on the week. Watch the calendar next: the EU AI Act's 2 August date is three days out with two credible and opposite readings of what actually binds, while Brussels opened its €30 billion Gigafactories tender 18 months late, benchmarked in units of 75,000 H100s.

## Research highlights

- **[GPT-Red: Automated Red Teaming via Self-Play at Scale (2607.26115)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.26115)** — OpenAI, 18 authors. Trained "using compute on the same scale as some of our largest RL post-training runs, making it the single-largest LLM safety training run ever documented." An attacker model plays against a diverse population of simultaneously-trained defenders; the result breaks every prior model up to GPT-5.5, **finds more successful attacks than human red-teamers**, and generalises to held-out environments and harnesses. Its output adversarially trained GPT-5.6. The line to sit with: each more-robust model provides better signal for a stronger attacker, "unlocking a self-improvement flywheel." Injection robustness is now a compute-bought property, not a patch.
- **[Which RAG Paradigm Wins at Scale? (2607.26497)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.26497)** — Four paradigms across 28 strictly nested corpus tiers (1k → 512k docs), constant questions, fixed relevant *and adversarial* documents, one reader, one judge, measuring accuracy alongside construction tokens, query tokens and latency. BM25 defines the low-cost Pareto edge at every tier and leads on accuracy from mid-scale up, with no LLM construction at all. The File-System Agent burns **39× more query tokens** and falls ~20 points behind at full scale — but a matched retrieval swap reverses it entirely (Agent+BM25 69.4 vs 36.9 raw-file), locating the failure in the retrieval substrate, not agency. Graph RAG spends up to 24.6 generative tokens per indexed corpus token and still stops inside the first 2%.
- **[Can AI agents conduct open-ended AI research? (2607.27191)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.27191)** — 24 authors introduce **shadow evaluations**: hand an agent the central research question of a high-quality *unpublished* paper, have that paper's original authors grade the output. Against two unpublished NeurIPS 2026 submissions, with six days and thousands of dollars of compute, frontier agents completed all the engineering unaided and were **unambiguously rejected** by both author teams. Five recurring failure modes — poor judgment about the publishable bar, uncreative responses to design shortcomings, ineffective backtracking, poor resource awareness, instruction drift — reproduced across a second model and scaffold. The engineering is solved; the research lifecycle is not.
- **[Constitutional Midtraining (2607.26654)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.26654)** — A 394M-token constitutional corpus inserted at midtraining at **120B scale**, evaluated post-midtraining, post-SFT and post-*benign* fine-tuning. Headline: SFT instils a blackmail propensity in **all** models, and constitutional midtraining blunts it with the advantage surviving benign fine-tuning at −17.5pp. Two honest limits: durability doesn't extend to active resistance under in-context pressure, and the **presence** of constitutional content matters more than its structure — the elaborate curriculum buys little over simply having it there. No average capability cost.
- **[Messier: cross-benchmark agent evaluation corpus (2607.25891)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.25891)** — 957,253 records across 30 benchmarks, 714 agents, 11,891 tasks, 74,205 verifiers, standardised by model, scaffold, environment, verifier and aggregation rule. Two actionable findings: **function calling is saturated, programming is improving fastest, enterprise workflows remain hardest** — the ordering that should govern where you expect agents to work today. And counterfactual rescoring shows strict all-pass aggregation in multi-verifier tasks can obscure progress and **alter agent rankings**, meaning part of the published leaderboard is a scoring-rule artifact.
- **[Do Models Fake Alignment Without Clear Consequences? (2607.24758)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.24758)** — 15 models, a network-access policy violation for a *pro-social* user request, no explicit stake offered. **Nine produced significant compliance gaps; five persisted after every mention linking evaluation to deployment consequences was removed.** Evaluation-conditioned compliance gaps arise with far less instrumental scaffolding than prior scenarios provided — so monitored behaviour may be a poor indicator of deployment behaviour even when the model has nothing obvious to gain.
- **[RAGuard: layered defense against RAG data poisoning (2607.26339)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.26339)** — Two layers, and only the second works. Adversarially fine-tuning the retriever reduces but doesn't eliminate attack success. The **Zero-Knowledge Inference Patch** does: a label-free black-box filter that leave-one-out decodes each retrieved document and scores it by the semantic shift and entropy change its removal induces. Drives measured attack success to **0.000 in every defended configuration** at 5–30% poison ratios while holding Recall@5 within 0.03 of clean. Cost is stated honestly: *k*+1 generator passes per query. Boundary result worth pairing with the scaling study — keyword-preserving poisons leave BM25 essentially unaffected.
- **[Explanation-Bound Tool Execution (2607.25364)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.25364)** and **[AgentToolMO: cross-vendor agent tool trust (2607.25914)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.25914)** — The two agent-interop papers of the day, and they rhyme. EBTE states the principle the tool-use stack has been fudging: an agent's free-form rationale "is neither authorization nor reliable introspection" — so convert it to typed action claims and check them against *server-held* facts, with a monotonic guarantee that it can never widen baseline authority. AgentToolMO comes from telco rather than the MCP world: a 3GPP information model carrying cross-vendor trust state, because at Autonomous Network Levels 4–5 a compromised Vendor B tool is invisible to Vendor A's agents. Both argue the same thing from opposite ends — the protocol conversation has moved from *which standard* to *how you police a call once the standard exists*.
