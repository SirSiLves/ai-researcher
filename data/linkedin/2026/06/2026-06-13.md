## Zero Trust for AI Agents Needs an Identity Layer
**Source:** Patrick Parker (Founder/CEO, EmpowerID) · 2026-06-07 · [linkedin.com/pulse/zero-trust-ai-agents-needs-identity-layer…](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/zero-trust-ai-agents-needs-identity-layer-patrick-parker-1jkle)

A response to Anthropic's "Zero Trust for AI agents" paper, arguing Zero Trust is the baseline but insufficient because agents *act* with delegated authority. Core argument ("AIdentity"): the control point must move from login to the material action, and the proof point from logs to signed receipts.

Key points relevant to MCP/agent governance:
- **The actor is split** across human principal, agent, model, policy engine, approver, credential vault, executor, and downstream system — collapsing these into one "who did this?" loses attribution.
- **MCP tool catalogs are authorization surfaces**, not just developer conveniences — tool descriptions/schemas can influence the model before any formal invocation; cites MCP spec's own security considerations (untrusted annotations, input validation, audit logging) as necessary but not sufficient.
- **"On behalf of" is overloaded** — FOR ME (pursuing my goal) vs AS ME (delegated authority) vs BY ME (I approved) are distinct; conflating them becomes "permission laundering," especially when agents call sub-agents (which should get derived, purpose-bound, time-bound authority).
- **Agents shouldn't hold credentials** — "least exposure" pattern: agent requests an action, policy authorizes, secure execution boundary uses the credential, agent gets only the result.
- **Continuous (action-time) authorization** is needed because dangerous intent doesn't exist at login — a new time-of-check/time-of-use problem.
- **Logs are not proof** — material actions should emit tamper-evident receipts proving authority, delegation, schema integrity, credential custody, and outcome.

Vendor patterns (EmpowerNow ARIA: OAuth Vault, signed receipts) referenced but the architectural argument stands on its own.

## Building an Agentic AI Workflow Using MCP: What Worked and What Didn't
**Source:** Prasan Cumar Nagendra Babu (Head of AI CoE) · 2026-06-09 · [linkedin.com/pulse/building-agentic-ai-workflow-using-mcp…](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/building-agentic-ai-workflow-using-mcp-what-worked-nagendra-babu-hhibc)

A practitioner retrospective on six weeks building a production customer-onboarding agent on Model Context Protocol (compressing a 3–5 day, six-system process to under 4 hours with human-in-loop for exceptions).

**What worked:** MCP's common interface forced organizational clarity (teams finally documented what their systems actually do); the agent picked up a newly registered third-party ID-verification tool with no changes to core logic ("agility at the tool layer without fragility at the intelligence layer"); genuine multi-system reasoning in one loop; dramatically higher-quality human escalations (structured briefs vs vague flags).

**What didn't:** ambiguous tools ("check_customer_status" doing three things) produced "confident incorrectness" — quietly wrong decisions; no "undo" in an agentic loop (an agent sent a welcome email before a compliance check completed because a dependency wasn't explicit); context windows are a *strategic* constraint requiring explicit summarization steps; and trust/governance/auditability were afterthoughts that should be designed in first.

Takeaways: design tools like APIs for humans (clear names, examples, when-NOT-to-use guidance), build the audit layer before the first production tool call, define the human-in-the-loop boundary on day one, and treat context as the scarce resource.

## EU AI Act High-Risk Classification: What It Means for Your Organization
**Source:** Harmeet Soni (Enterprise Architect, AI Strategy & Governance) · 2026-06-11 · [linkedin.com/pulse/eu-ai-act-high-risk-classification…](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/eu-ai-act-high-risk-classification-what-means-your-harmeet-soni-truge)

Practical translation of the EU AI Act's high-risk framework into governance questions. Notes recent timeline updates may push many standalone high-risk obligations toward **December 2027**, but argues classification is a present-day decision.

- **Risk tiers:** unacceptable (prohibited), high (allowed with strict pre/post-deployment requirements), limited (transparency), minimal (little/no obligation). Misclassifying high-risk as limited/minimal carries regulatory, operational, reputational, and financial exposure.
- **Annex III high-risk categories** relevant to enterprises: critical infrastructure, employment/workforce management (hiring, screening, performance, monitoring), education/training, essential private/public services (credit, insurance, benefits, eligibility, fraud), plus healthcare, law enforcement, migration, justice, democratic processes.
- **Decision framework:** (1) explicitly listed in Annex III? (2) regulated product/safety component? (3) makes/supports decisions affecting people? (4) document the rationale.
- **High-risk requirements** span risk management, data governance, technical documentation, logging/audit trails, transparency, human oversight, accuracy, robustness, cybersecurity.
- **ISO/IEC 42001** positioned as the implementation bridge — not auto-required for EU AI Act compliance but a practical operational framework. Key reminder: risk is primarily about the **use case/context**, not the model; the same model can be low- or high-risk depending on deployment. Extraterritorial reach applies to non-EU orgs whose systems are placed on, used in, or produce outputs affecting people in the EU.
