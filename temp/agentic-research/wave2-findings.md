**PRO arguments:**
- ✅ Shadow AI is already inside Raiffeisen — the question is not whether developers use AI but whether the bank can see and govern it. With ~98% of enterprises affected, ~68% of employees using unauthorized tools, and ~90% of AI logins on personal/non-SSO accounts, a 'do nothing / informal ban' posture means the bank is blind to where its source code and customer data are going.
- ✅ There is a hard, independent dollar figure: IBM puts the shadow-AI breach premium at +USD 670K per breach, with shadow AI implicated in 1 in 5 breaches and 97% of AI-breached orgs lacking access controls. For a FINMA-regulated bank, a single source-code or customer-PII leak via a consumer chatbot carries regulatory, reputational and contractual-third-party-risk consequences far beyond that average.
- ✅ Source code is specifically the leakage hotspot — it is the #1 GenAI data-policy violation in Netskope's telemetry and ~26-30% of all sensitive exposures in Harmonic's 22.4M-prompt dataset. With several hundred developers, Raiffeisen's exposure is concentrated exactly where the risk is highest, making a dedicated, focused engineering unit the natural owner of the mitigation.
- ✅ Sanctioning beats banning, with evidence: providing an approved tool is reported to cut unauthorized use by up to ~89%, while bans demonstrably drive usage underground (JPMorgan could not even count its own ChatGPT users before banning it). A governed agentic platform with SSO, audit logging, enterprise data-retention (no training on prompts) and DLP converts an invisible risk into a controlled, observable one — and Samsung shows that an ungoverned 'allow' is just as dangerous as a reversed ban.
- ✅ A sanctioned platform is the only way to control the new agentic supply chain. Banning AI does nothing about developers adding unvetted MCP servers and browser extensions locally — a single malicious MCP server (as seen in 2025) can exfiltrate every credential it aggregates. An official platform with a vetted MCP/extension allowlist, signed releases and code review is a governance capability that simply cannot exist in a shadow-AI world.
- ✅ The dedicated personnel unit is justified by the governance gap itself: only 37% of orgs have shadow-AI detection policies and 63% of breached orgs lack mature AI governance. Tooling alone does not close this — it requires a focused team to run discovery, set the allowlist, monitor telemetry, manage data-residency/EU-AI-Act/FADP obligations, and keep the sanctioned platform good enough that developers have no reason to defect.

**CONTRA arguments:**
- ❌ Much of the headline data is vendor-sourced and self-interested. Harmonic, LayerX, Cyberhaven, Netskope and Nudge all sell shadow-AI detection/governance products, so their prevalence figures (77% paste, 90% non-SSO, etc.) should be treated as directional, not audited. The most defensible numbers come from IBM/Ponemon (independent methodology) and Gartner; the pitch should lead with those and use vendor figures only as corroboration.
- ❌ The strongest single 'govern-beats-ban' stat (~89% drop in unauthorized use when a sanctioned tool is provided) traces only to a secondary outlet (Healthcare Brew) and lacks a rigorous primary study. It should be presented as illustrative, not as a guaranteed outcome — over-claiming it to a C-level audience risks undermining credibility.
- ❌ The Samsung case is from 2023 and is sometimes overstated/contested in detail (some reporting questions the exact 'three incidents' framing). It remains the best-known proof point but is not 2025-2026 data; rely on it for the mechanism (code pasted into a consumer chatbot) rather than as current evidence.
- ❌ A sanctioned platform reduces but does not eliminate the risk — it can create a false sense of security. Developers can still paste secrets, the platform itself becomes a high-value target, and governance overhead (DLP false positives, latency, restrictive allowlists) can be bad enough that developers route around it back to personal accounts. The 'keep it good enough that they don't defect' condition is a real, ongoing operational burden, not a one-time purchase.
- ❌ For a Swiss bank, the sanctioned-platform decision interacts with data residency and third-party risk: an 'official' SaaS AI platform still sends prompts to a vendor (often US-hosted), raising FADP/DSG, EU AI Act and FINMA outsourcing-governance questions. The governance unit's first job may actually be choosing a deployment model (in-tenant, EU-region, or self-hosted models) — which adds cost and complexity the simple 'just sanction it' narrative glosses over.
- ❌ Prevalence statistics conflate casual ChatGPT use with high-risk developer behavior. A board could reasonably ask whether the marginal risk reduction justifies a dedicated unit versus extending existing DLP/CASB and IAM controls to cover AI — i.e., the build-a-team conclusion does not follow automatically from the leakage statistics alone and needs a separate cost/benefit case.
